Module 01

Nouns

A noun names a person, place, thing, substance, creature, idea, quality or state.

1. What Is a Noun?

A noun is a word that functions as the name of an entity, concept, substance, place, person or group. Nouns commonly serve as subjects, objects, complements and objects of prepositions.

Core structural rule: A noun phrase normally has a noun as its head and may contain determiners, adjectives and modifiers. the extremely talented student
Subject The teacher explained the rule.
Object We completed the assignment.
Complement She is a doctor.
Object of preposition The book is on the table.

Proper Nouns

A proper noun identifies a particular person, place, organization, day, month, title or other unique entity. In standard English it normally begins with a capital letter.

Examples India, Ravi, Monday, Asia, Amazon River, Oxford University

Common Nouns

A common noun names a general member of a class rather than one uniquely identified individual.

Examples country, student, river, university, animal, city

Concrete Nouns

Concrete nouns refer to entities that can be experienced through the physical senses.

book tree bell apple rain

Abstract Nouns

Abstract nouns name ideas, qualities, emotions, conditions or concepts that are not normally perceived as physical objects.

honesty freedom beauty anger wisdom

Countable Nouns

Countable nouns can occur in singular and plural forms and can be counted individually.

one book → two books → many books
Singular countable nouns normally require a determiner when used as ordinary singular noun phrases: I bought a book. The book is interesting. I bought three books.

Uncountable Nouns

Uncountable nouns normally refer to substances, materials, abstract masses or concepts treated as undivided quantities.

Material water, rice, sugar, furniture
Abstract advice, information, knowledge, patience
Important: Standard English normally says some information, not some informations, when information means the general mass concept.

Collective Nouns

A collective noun names a group considered as a unit.

Examples team, committee, family, audience, flock, army, class

Collective nouns can create agreement questions because the writer may conceptualize the group as one unit or, depending on dialect and context, emphasize individual members.

Compound Nouns

A compound noun consists of two or more lexical elements functioning together as a noun.

Form Examples Plural pattern
Closed notebook, football, bedroom notebooks, footballs, bedrooms
Hyphenated mother-in-law, passer-by mothers-in-law, passers-by
Open school bus, post office school buses, post offices
Diagnostic strategy: First determine what the word names. Then determine whether the noun is proper/common, concrete/abstract, countable/uncountable, collective or compound. These classifications can overlap.

Module 02

Verbs

Verbs express actions, events, states and relationships and form the grammatical core of clauses.

Action Verbs

Action verbs describe physical or mental activities, events or processes.

Physical The athlete ran.
Mental She considered the evidence.

Stative Verbs

Stative verbs describe states rather than dynamic activities. They frequently express possession, perception, knowledge, emotion, opinion or relationship.

know believe own belong prefer understand seem
Usage caution: Stative verbs often resist the progressive form in their ordinary stative meanings. However, some can become dynamic when their meaning changes. I think you are correct. I am thinking about the problem.

Transitive Verbs

A transitive verb takes a direct object in the relevant construction.

Subject + Verb + Direct Object The student + solved + the problem.

Intransitive Verbs

An intransitive verb does not take a direct object.

Subject + Verb The baby slept.

Some verbs can be transitive in one construction and intransitive in another.

The door opened. ← intransitive The student opened the door. ← transitive

Auxiliary / Helping Verbs

Auxiliary verbs combine with lexical verbs to express tense, aspect, voice, emphasis or related grammatical meanings.

Auxiliary Major functions Example
be Progressive aspect, passive voice She is reading. The door was opened.
have Perfect aspect They have finished.
do Questions, negatives, emphasis Do you agree? I do agree.

Linking Verbs

Linking verbs connect the subject with a subject complement that identifies or describes it.

Subject + Linking Verb + Subject Complement The sky + became + dark. She + is + intelligent.

Common linking verbs include forms of be, become, seem, appear, remain, feel, look, smell, taste, sound and grow when used to express a resulting state or sensory condition.

Modal Verbs

Modal auxiliaries express meanings such as ability, possibility, permission, obligation, advice, prediction and necessity.

Modal Common meanings Example
can ability, permission, possibility She can swim.
could past ability, polite request, possibility Could you help me?
may permission, possibility It may rain.
might possibility He might arrive later.
shall formal future, suggestion Shall we begin?
should advice, expectation You should revise.
will future, willingness I will help.
would politeness, conditionals, habitual past Would you like tea?
must necessity, strong inference You must wear a helmet.
Modal structure: Subject + Modal + Base Verb She can sing. They should study. He must leave. A modal is followed by the base form of the lexical verb, not an ordinary third-person singular -s form.

Module 03

Adjectives — Exhaustive Treatment

Adjectives modify nouns and pronouns by describing, limiting, identifying or quantifying them.

Descriptive / Qualitative Adjectives

Descriptive adjectives express a quality or characteristic of a noun.

Examples a bright student, a careful driver, an ancient temple, a beautiful painting

Quantitative Adjectives

Quantitative adjectives indicate amount or quantity. They are commonly associated with nouns whose quantity is being described.

much little enough some any sufficient

Demonstrative Adjectives

Demonstratives identify nouns in relation to distance, context or discourse.

this book that book these books those books
Number agreement: this/that normally pair with singular or uncountable noun phrases; these/those pair with plural countable noun phrases.

Possessive Adjectives

Possessive determiners indicate a relationship such as ownership or association.

Person Possessive determiner Example
First singularmymy book
Second singular/pluralyouryour book
Third singular masculinehishis book
Third singular feminineherher book
Third singular non-humanitsits cover
First pluralourour book
Third pluraltheirtheir book

Interrogative Adjectives

Interrogative words can function as determiners before nouns in questions.

Which book do you prefer? What subject are you studying? Whose notebook is this?

Distributive Adjectives

Distributive determiners refer to members of a group individually rather than simply treating the group as one mass.

each every either neither
Agreement: Each and every are normally followed by a singular countable noun and take singular agreement: Every student is ready.

Proper Adjectives

A proper adjective is derived from a proper noun and generally retains capitalization.

Examples Indian cuisine, Shakespearean drama, Victorian architecture, American literature

Indefinite Adjectives

Indefinite determiners describe nouns without identifying an exact individual or quantity.

some any many several few various numerous certain

Order of Adjectives

When multiple adjectives precede one noun, English has a conventional ordering pattern. Real-world usage can vary for stylistic reasons, but the following matrix is a useful default.

Order Category Example
1Quantity / Numberthree
2Opinionbeautiful
3Sizelarge
4Physical Qualitysmooth
5Shaperound
6Ageold
7Colorred
8OriginIndian
9Materialwooden
10Typeelectric
11Purposecooking
12Nounstove
three beautiful large smooth round old red Indian wooden cooking bowls
Memory pattern: Quantity → Opinion → Size → Physical Quality → Shape → Age → Color → Origin → Material → Type → Purpose → Noun.

Module 04

Adverbs

Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, other adverbs, phrases or clauses.

Adverbs of Manner

These describe how an action happens.

The athlete ran quickly.
She answered politely.
He worked carefully.

Adverbs of Time

Time adverbs identify when an action occurs or its temporal relationship to another event.

today yesterday tomorrow soon already later recently

Adverbs of Place

Place adverbs identify location or direction.

here there everywhere outside nearby away

Adverbs of Frequency

Frequency adverbs indicate how often something happens.

Frequency expression Example placement
alwaysShe always studies.
usuallyHe usually walks.
oftenThey often practise.
sometimesShe sometimes sings.
rarelyHe rarely complains.
neverThey never smoke.
Placement: Frequency adverbs commonly occur before the main lexical verb but after the verb be. She always studies. She is always punctual.

Adverbs of Degree

Degree adverbs indicate intensity, extent or amount.

very extremely quite rather too almost enough
very tall extremely difficult almost finished too expensive good enough

Module 05

Pronouns

Pronouns substitute for or refer to noun phrases, helping speakers avoid unnecessary repetition.

Personal Pronouns

Personal pronouns encode person, number and, in some forms, case or gender distinctions.

Role Singular Plural
SubjectI, you, he, she, itwe, you, they
Objectme, you, him, her, itus, you, them

Relative Pronouns

Relative pronouns introduce relative clauses and connect them to antecedent noun phrases.

who → usually people whom → object form in formal usage whose → possession which → things and animals in many contexts that → people or things in many defining clauses
The student who won the prize smiled.

Demonstrative Pronouns

Demonstrative pronouns stand independently rather than modifying a following noun.

This is excellent. Those are mine.

Indefinite Pronouns

Indefinite pronouns refer to people, things or quantities without specifying exact identities.

someone anyone everyone nobody something anything everything nothing many few several

Reflexive Pronouns

Reflexive pronouns normally refer back to an antecedent within the same clause.

myself yourself / yourselves himself herself itself ourselves themselves
The child hurt himself.

Intensive Pronouns

The same -self forms can be intensive when they emphasize a noun phrase rather than serving as the required object.

The principal herself announced the result.

Interrogative Pronouns

Interrogative pronouns introduce questions.

who whom whose which what

Reciprocal Pronouns

Reciprocal pronouns express a mutual relationship.

each other one another
The two friends helped each other.

Module 06

Prepositions & Conjunctions

Prepositions establish relationships; conjunctions connect words, phrases and clauses.

Prepositions

A preposition typically introduces a complement and expresses a relationship involving location, time, direction, cause, means or another semantic relation.

Relationship Examples
Placein, on, under, beside, between
Timeat, on, in, before, after, during
Directionto, into, toward, through
Causebecause of, due to
Meansby, with

Prepositional Phrase

Preposition + Complement under + the table after + the lesson with + my friend
The keys are under the table.

Conjunctions

Coordinating Conjunctions — FANBOYS

The mnemonic FANBOYS represents the seven coordinating conjunctions.

Letter Conjunction Typical relationship
Fforreason
Aandaddition
Nnornegative alternative
Bbutcontrast
Ooralternative
Yyetcontrast
Ssoresult
Independent clause + , + FANBOYS + independent clause The test was difficult, but the students remained calm.

Subordinating Conjunctions

Subordinating conjunctions introduce dependent clauses and establish relationships such as time, cause, condition, contrast and purpose.

because although if unless when while before after since although whereas
Although it was raining, we continued the match. We continued the match although it was raining.

Correlative Conjunctions

Correlative conjunctions operate in pairs and connect grammatically parallel elements.

Pair Example
either...orEither Maya or Riya will present.
neither...norNeither the teacher nor the students were late.
both...andBoth grammar and vocabulary matter.
not only...but alsoShe is not only clever but also patient.
whether...orWhether you stay or leave, call me.
Parallelism: The grammatical forms linked by correlative conjunctions should normally match. She likes reading and writing. NOT: She likes reading and to write.

Module 08

The Twelve English Tenses

English tense and aspect combinations allow speakers to locate events in time and describe their completion, duration or progression.

Four aspect patterns: Simple describes events or states generally; Continuous emphasizes an ongoing process; Perfect relates an earlier event to a later reference point; Perfect Continuous emphasizes duration or ongoing activity up to a reference point.

1. Present Simple

Habits, routines, facts, states and schedules.

Subject + base verb / -s form
Example: She studies every evening.

2. Present Continuous

Actions happening around the present time or temporary situations.

Subject + am/is/are + V-ing
Example: She is studying now.

3. Present Perfect

Past events connected to the present.

Subject + has/have + past participle
Example: She has finished the work.

4. Present Perfect Continuous

An activity continuing up to now or recently completed with emphasis on duration.

Subject + has/have been + V-ing
Example: She has been studying for two hours.

5. Past Simple

Completed events at a past time.

Subject + past form
Example: She studied yesterday.

6. Past Continuous

An activity in progress at a particular past time or interrupted activity.

Subject + was/were + V-ing
Example: She was studying at eight.

7. Past Perfect

An event completed before another past reference point.

Subject + had + past participle
Example: She had finished before dinner.

8. Past Perfect Continuous

Duration of an activity before a past reference point.

Subject + had been + V-ing
Example: She had been studying for two hours.

9. Future Simple

Predictions, decisions, promises and future events.

Subject + will + base verb
Example: She will study tomorrow.

10. Future Continuous

An activity that will be in progress at a future time.

Subject + will be + V-ing
Example: She will be studying at eight.

11. Future Perfect

An event that will be completed before a future reference point.

Subject + will have + past participle
Example: She will have finished by Friday.

12. Future Perfect Continuous

Duration continuing up to a future reference point.

Subject + will have been + V-ing
Example: She will have been studying for three hours.

Tense Comparison

Pattern Past Present Future
Simple I worked. I work. I will work.
Continuous I was working. I am working. I will be working.
Perfect I had worked. I have worked. I will have worked.
Perfect Continuous I had been working. I have been working. I will have been working.

Module 09

Advanced Syntax Rules

Master difficult agreement, modifier and voice structures that frequently distinguish accurate writing from grammatical errors.

Subject–Verb Agreement

The finite verb must agree with its grammatical subject in number and person where English marks that distinction.

Agreement with intervening phrases

The box of old photographs is upstairs. NOT: The box of old photographs are upstairs.

The head of the subject noun phrase is box, not photographs.

Each and Every

Each student is responsible. Every player has a uniform.

Either...or / Neither...nor

In careful formal agreement, when the coordinated subjects differ in number, agreement may follow the nearer subject in constructions such as these.

Neither the teacher nor the students are ready. Neither the students nor the teacher is ready.

Amounts and Units

Expressions involving amounts can take singular agreement when conceived as one total quantity.

Ten kilometres is a long distance. Five years is a long time.

Collective Nouns

Collective nouns require attention to meaning and dialect. American English often favors singular agreement when the group is treated as a unit, while British usage may allow plural agreement when the members are emphasized.

Misplaced Modifiers

A modifier should appear close enough to the word it logically modifies that the intended relationship is unmistakable.

Problematic She almost drove her children to school every day.

Depending on intended meaning, almost may appear to modify the wrong element.

Clearer She drove her children to school almost every day.

Dangling Modifiers

A dangling modifier lacks a logical grammatical subject to modify.

Dangling Walking through the park, the trees looked beautiful.
Corrected Walking through the park, I thought the trees looked beautiful.
Modifier test: Ask “Who is performing the action in the introductory modifier?” The grammatical subject immediately following the modifier should normally be that logical actor.

Active and Passive Voice

In active voice, the grammatical subject is typically the agent or actor. In passive voice, the grammatical subject corresponds to the active sentence's object or affected participant.

Active Structure

Subject + Verb + Object The student solved the problem.

Passive Structure

Object + be + Past Participle + (by + Agent) The problem was solved by the student.

Transformation Procedure

  1. Identify the active sentence's direct object.
  2. Move that object into subject position.
  3. Select the appropriate tense of be.
  4. Use the main verb's past participle.
  5. Add the original subject with by if the agent is relevant.
Active tense Passive pattern Example
Present simple am/is/are + V3 The report is written.
Past simple was/were + V3 The report was written.
Present continuous am/is/are being + V3 The report is being written.
Past continuous was/were being + V3 The report was being written.
Present perfect has/have been + V3 The report has been written.
Past perfect had been + V3 The report had been written.
Future will be + V3 The report will be written.
Key principle: Passive voice is not simply “putting a sentence backwards.” The auxiliary be carries the tense/aspect information while the lexical verb appears as a past participle.
